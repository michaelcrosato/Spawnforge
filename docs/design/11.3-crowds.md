# Design: GPU cost and crowds (milestone 11.3)

Two pieces. A benchmark that measures what creatures cost a GPU: the agent's sandbox renders on
the CPU (SwiftShader), so its numbers say little, and the owner runs it on a mid-range laptop.
And crowds: distant creatures drawn instanced, per species, level of detail and mesh, posed on
the GPU from their baked clips, so hundreds of them cost a few draw calls and no per-bone work on
the CPU. Plan 2 rates it xhigh (confirmed): skinning from textures in TSL is new code, and
performance can only be measured on real hardware. The oracle for the crowd is exactness: an
instanced creature must be posed as the same creature drawn by its own skeleton at the baked
level of detail.

Rule one: nothing changes unless asked. Crowds are opt-in (`createBestiary({ crowds: true })`);
without them every creature draws as before.

## Decisions

| # | Question | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Who is in a crowd | A creature at the baked level of detail (beyond `lodDistance`), alive and playing a baked clip: walking, standing, swimming or cruising in the air. Takeoffs, landings, actions and dying stay at full detail, as the baked level of detail already keeps them; a corpse keeps its own mesh (it lies still and costs nothing per frame). Membership is decided before each creature's update, so a member skips posing its bones. |
| 2 | Opt-in | `createBestiary({ crowds: true })` and `scene.add(bestiary.crowd)` once (a `Group` the bestiary owns). A member hides its own object (`crowded` is true). Its bones are posed only when something reads them: `socket` and `hitCapsules` pose it first. |
| 3 | The bone texture | Per species: a float RGBA texture (`clipRows`), one row per frame of every clip the baked level of detail plays (idle and every gait), four texels per bone (three's `Skeleton.boneMatrices` for that frame, column by column: bone world × inverse bind) and one for the root's position. Posed by the same code path the runtime's `applyClip` follows, at the origin facing +Z, and computed by three itself (`skeleton.update()`), so a row is exactly what the skeleton would upload. |
| 4 | The draw | `CrowdDraw`: one per species, holding the species' own materials (so its shaders compile once) over its geometry: a skin and a parts mesh per level of detail (11.2), sharing the full meshes' vertex attributes, and one eyes and one membranes mesh, the same at every level. Each is drawn once per member it covers (three r186 instances any object with `count`, as the fur already does). The materials' `positionNode` skins each vertex from the texture: per bone, the two frames' matrices lerped by the member's blend, weighted; then turned about the root by the member's rotation and moved by its offset, as `applyClip` moves and turns the root; the normal likewise (`normalLocal`, as three's skinning assigns it). Three's node materials apply `positionNode` after instancing, so the node does the instance's part itself. Per member, three texels in an instance texture, grouped by level: offset and first row, rotation, second row and blend; each level's meshes find their run through a per-object uniform (`userData.crowdBase`). One upload a frame. |
| 5 | Exactness | On a frame the crowd's pose is the skeleton's: a Node test compares every hundredth vertex with three's CPU skinning of the posed bones (under 0.01 mm), and a render test draws a creature both ways and requires the same pixels (fewer than 0.1% differing by more than 2 of 255). Between frames the per-creature path slerps bone rotations and the crowd lerps matrices: a few millimetres on a 1 m wolf in a gallop, under a pixel at the distances crowds are drawn (a pixel is about 2.5 cm at 27 m on a 1080-pixel screen). |
| 6 | What crowds leave out | Breathing (the swell along normals) and fur shells (already hidden at the baked level of detail). Blinks are in the clips. Patterns pulse on the crowd's clock, not each member's. |
| 7 | Making draws | A species joins a crowd once its levels of detail are made (11.2), and makes its one draw then, with every level in it, so a member changing level costs no new shader. The draw holds at least twice its members when made, in multiples of 64; when the crowd outgrows it, the draw is made again. |
| 8 | The bench | `pnpm bench` runs the render page's bench headless: three scenes, each for 300 frames after 60 to warm up (shaders, levels of detail and crowd draws are made then): 50 creatures near the camera at full motion (all 32 examples in turn at medium quality, walking to random spots, turning, acting now and then, with fur and membranes), 500 distant ones, and the same 500 drawn as crowds. Per scene: the median and 95th-percentile frame time (update plus render as the CPU submits it) and update time, GPU time per frame from timestamp queries where the backend has them, draw calls, triangles, how many are at full motion, crowded, and at each mesh level, and the spawn time. Each frame starts on the next animation frame, as a game's loop does, so the page runs its tasks between frames (a tight loop of awaited renders never did, and the runtime's first import of `bake/lod` never finished). Headless it runs on WebGL 2 in SwiftShader: numbers recorded, not judged; CI runs `--small` (10, 60 and 60 creatures, 20 frames). `pnpm bench --open` serves the page for the owner's browser (`?bench`), on WebGPU where it has it, which shows the numbers and saves them as JSON for `docs/poc/`. |

## Done when

The bench runs headless in CI (numbers recorded, not gated), the owner's numbers are in
`docs/poc/` or marked as awaiting the owner's run, and a render test shows instanced crowds posed
exactly as the per-creature baked level of detail.

## Checks

`three`: rows match the skeleton on a frame (Node), the bestiary puts a distant creature in its
species' crowd, hides its own object, and poses its bones when its sockets are read. `render`: a
wolf (fur), a beetle (chitin, glow) and a bat flying (membranes) drawn by their crowds give the
same pixels as by their skeletons; the bench's small run completes.
